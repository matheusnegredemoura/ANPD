apoio_EN15A: For years, the dominant assumption in machine learning was simple: the more data, the better the model. Developers collected everything they could, reasoning that information which looked useless today might prove valuable tomorrow.
Data protection law challenges that habit. The principle of data minimisation requires organisations to process only the data that are adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary for a specified purpose. A vague hope of future usefulness is not, in itself, a purpose.
Some engineers had feared that this would cripple innovation. In practice, many teams have found that smaller, carefully curated datasets can produce models that are not only easier to secure but also less prone to reproducing errors and biases buried in the excess. Minimisation, it turns out, can be good engineering as well as good law.

apoio_EN15B: A cookie banner offers two buttons. One, large and brightly coloured, says “Accept all”. The other, small and grey, says “Manage options” and leads to a series of screens in which every setting must be switched off by hand. Nothing in the design is technically false, yet the outcome is predictable: most people click the first button.
Designers call such techniques “dark patterns”. Regulators increasingly treat them as a problem of consent rather than of aesthetics. If refusing is made much harder than accepting, the choice can hardly be described as free, and consent obtained this way may be invalid.
The remedy is not to ban persuasion altogether. It is to ensure that the options are presented with comparable prominence, so that users who would rather say no are not punished for it with extra effort.
