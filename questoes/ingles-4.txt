apoio_EN4A: Few issues unite parents and legislators as quickly as the safety of children online. Over the last few years, a growing number of countries have passed laws requiring digital platforms to verify, or at least estimate, the age of their users before granting access to certain content. On the surface, the idea seems uncontroversial: if a website sells alcohol or shows violent material, it should make sure that its visitors are adults.
The practical picture is less simple. The most reliable ways of checking age, such as uploading an identity document or scanning a face, require collecting precisely the kind of sensitive information that privacy advocates would rather keep out of corporate hands. Less intrusive methods, such as asking users to type their date of birth, are trivially easy to bypass.
Regulators have therefore started to talk about proportionality. A service aimed at small children, or one that hosts gambling, may justify stricter checks; a news website probably does not. What most experts agree on is that age assurance should not become an excuse to build vast databases of identity documents, which would themselves become attractive targets for criminals.
apoio_EN4B: Most people have never heard of the companies that know the most about them. Data brokers rarely deal with consumers directly. Instead, they gather information from public records, loyalty programmes, mobile applications and other brokers, combine it into detailed profiles and sell those profiles to advertisers, insurers, landlords and, in some countries, government agencies.
Because the trade happens out of sight, individuals find it hard to exercise rights that the law formally grants them. How can someone ask a company to delete their data if they do not know that the company exists? Some legislators have responded by creating public registries in which brokers must enrol, while others have proposed a single mechanism through which a person could send one deletion request to every registered broker at once.
The industry argues that its products help prevent fraud and allow small businesses to reach customers they could not otherwise afford to find. Critics counter that the same profiles can be used to infer health conditions, financial distress or political views, and that consent obtained through lengthy terms of service, which almost nobody reads, is hardly meaningful.
apoio_EN4C: Data, unlike goods, do not stop at customs. A message sent from São Paulo to Lisbon may pass through servers in several other countries before reaching its destination, and a single cloud contract can spread a company's files across three continents. For data protection authorities, this raises a basic question: how can rights granted under national law survive once information leaves the national territory?
The answers vary, but most regimes share a common structure. Transfers are generally allowed to countries whose level of protection has been formally recognised as adequate. Where no such recognition exists, companies may rely on contractual safeguards, such as standard clauses approved by the regulator, or on binding rules adopted within a corporate group. A narrow set of exceptions, including the protection of a person's life, covers situations in which none of these instruments is available.
Adequacy decisions are attractive because they reduce paperwork, but they are also political. Negotiations can take years, and a decision may be reviewed if the foreign country changes its laws. Mutual recognition, in which two jurisdictions declare each other adequate, has become a diplomatic goal in its own right.
apoio_EN4D: Independent agencies were created on the premise that some decisions are too technical, or too tempting to manipulate, to be left to elected politicians. By granting officials fixed terms of office and protecting them from dismissal at will, legislators hoped to insulate regulation from the electoral cycle and to reassure investors that the rules would not change every four years.
Independence, however, does not guarantee impartiality. Economists have long warned of regulatory capture, a process through which an agency gradually comes to serve the interests of the industry it was meant to oversee. Capture rarely involves outright corruption. More often it results from subtler forces: regulators depend on companies for information, share their technical vocabulary and may hope to work for them after leaving office.
Several safeguards have been designed to counter these pressures. Cooling-off periods prevent former directors from joining regulated firms immediately after their terms end; public consultations open the rule-making process to consumers and civil society; and regulatory impact analyses force agencies to justify their choices with evidence rather than with the preferences of the loudest participants.
apoio_EN4E: Anyone who browses the web has met the banner: a box that slides up from the bottom of the screen, announces that the site values your privacy and offers a large, brightly coloured button to accept all cookies. The option to refuse, when it exists, is often hidden behind a second screen or written in pale grey letters.
Designers call these techniques dark patterns, interface choices that steer users towards decisions they would not otherwise make. They exploit well-documented features of human behaviour, such as our tendency to stick with default settings and our impatience when a task stands between us and the content we want.
Data protection authorities have begun to treat such designs as a legal problem rather than a matter of taste. If refusing is harder than accepting, they argue, consent cannot be considered freely given. Some regulators now expect the two options to appear on the same screen and with equal prominence. Others go further and question whether consent is the right legal basis at all for practices that most users neither understand nor want.
