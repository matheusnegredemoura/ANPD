apoio_EN6A: When a hospital is hit by ransomware, the damage is not measured only in money. Appointments are cancelled, ambulances are diverted to other facilities and doctors return to paper records they have not used for years. For this reason, attacks on healthcare have come to be seen less as a matter of corporate security and more as a threat to public safety.
Paying the ransom may look like the quickest way out, but it offers no guarantee. Criminals do not always hand over working keys, and those who pay may be targeted again, since they have shown that they are willing to pay. Many governments therefore discourage payment, although only a few have gone as far as forbidding it outright.
Experts agree that prevention is far cheaper than recovery. Regular backups kept offline, prompt installation of security updates and staff trained to recognise suspicious e-mails would have stopped a large share of the attacks reported in recent years. The problem is that such measures compete for funds with more visible priorities, such as new equipment and longer opening hours, and their benefits only become apparent when something goes wrong.
apoio_EN6B: For a long time, competition law and data protection were treated as separate worlds. Competition authorities looked at prices and market shares; privacy regulators looked at consent forms and security breaches. The rise of digital platforms has made this division harder to sustain.
Many online services are offered at a price of zero, so consumers do not pay with money. They pay with attention and with personal data. When a dominant company lowers the quality of its privacy terms, collecting more data than before without offering anything in return, some economists argue that this is the digital equivalent of raising prices.
Data can also work as a barrier to entry. A newcomer may build a better search engine or a cleverer social network, but without the volume of information accumulated by the incumbent over many years, its product may never become good enough to attract users. Proposals to address this problem include requiring large platforms to let users move their data to rival services and, in some cases, to share certain datasets with competitors.
Neither solution is free of risk. Data portability depends on users actually exercising the right, and forced data sharing could expose personal information to more companies than before, which is exactly what privacy law tries to prevent.
apoio_EN6C: Before a new rule is adopted, many governments now require the regulator to ask a simple question: is this rule worth what it costs? Regulatory impact assessment is the name given to the process of answering it in a structured way. The regulator describes the problem, considers alternative ways of addressing it, including doing nothing, and estimates the likely costs and benefits of each option.
In theory, the method protects citizens and businesses from rules that are poorly designed or that impose burdens out of proportion to their aims. In practice, its quality varies widely. Some assessments are prepared after the decision has already been taken, merely to justify it, and benefits such as privacy or dignity are notoriously hard to express in monetary terms.
Supporters of the practice respond that an imperfect estimate is still better than none. Even when numbers are uncertain, the exercise forces regulators to make their assumptions explicit, which allows others to challenge them. Public consultation on the draft assessment, they add, can bring to light information that officials would never have found on their own.
Increasingly, attention is turning to what happens after a rule takes effect. Reviewing whether a regulation has actually achieved its goals, and repealing those that have not, is the necessary complement to assessing them beforehand.
apoio_EN6D: The large language models behind today's chatbots were trained on enormous quantities of text gathered from the internet. Much of that material contains personal information: names, opinions, photographs and, occasionally, details that people would never have expected to end up inside a commercial product.
Developers usually argue that publicly available data may be used freely. Data protection authorities have been less convinced. In their view, the fact that information can be seen by anyone does not mean that it can be collected in bulk and reused for any purpose. The individuals concerned still have rights, and the company that collects the data must still identify a lawful basis for doing so.
Several authorities have suggested that legitimate interest may serve as that basis, but only if certain conditions are met. The company must show that its interest is real, that the collection is necessary and that the expected benefits are not outweighed by the risks to the people whose data are used. Measures such as excluding sensitive categories of data, respecting technical signals that ask crawlers to stay away and offering a simple way to object are often mentioned as part of that balancing exercise.
A further difficulty is that, once a model has been trained, removing a particular person's information from it is far from straightforward.
apoio_EN6E: Few ideas in data protection have generated as much controversy as the so-called right to be forgotten. In its best-known form, it allows individuals to ask search engines to stop displaying links to information about them that is inaccurate, outdated or no longer relevant. The original pages are not deleted; they simply become harder to find through a search on the person's name.
Supporters see the right as a way of letting people move on with their lives. A minor offence committed decades ago, a debt long since repaid or an embarrassing photograph from adolescence should not, in their view, follow someone forever. Critics fear that the same mechanism could be used by powerful figures to bury legitimate criticism and that private companies are being asked to make decisions that should belong to courts.
Courts that have recognised the right have stressed that it is not absolute. Each request must be weighed against the public's interest in access to information, which is especially strong when the person concerned plays a role in public life. Other courts have taken a more sceptical view, holding that the passage of time alone does not justify suppressing information that was lawfully published.
