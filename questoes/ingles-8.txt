apoio_EN8A: When offices emptied during the pandemic, many employers turned to software that promised to show whether staff working from home were really working. Some programs recorded keystrokes, others took screenshots at random intervals, and a few even switched on the laptop camera to confirm that the employee was sitting at the desk.
Managers defended these tools as a reasonable substitute for the oversight they had lost. Employees, however, often described the experience as exhausting. Knowing that every pause might be logged, they reported working longer hours while feeling less trusted. Several studies have found that intensive monitoring can actually lower productivity, since workers learn to perform activity for the software rather than to do their jobs well.
Data protection authorities have reminded employers that the employment relationship is unequal, which makes it difficult to rely on consent: a worker who fears losing a job is hardly free to refuse. Monitoring must instead be justified by a legitimate purpose, limited to what is necessary and explained to staff in advance. Covert surveillance, where it is allowed at all, is generally reserved for the investigation of specific suspicions of serious wrongdoing.
apoio_EN8B: Fitness bands and smartwatches now measure far more than steps. Many record heart rate through the night, estimate stress levels and detect irregular heart rhythms that may warrant a visit to a doctor. For users, this information can be genuinely useful; for the companies that sell the devices, it is also valuable in a different way.
Unlike data held by hospitals, the readings collected by consumer gadgets often fall outside the rules that protect medical records. In several jurisdictions, health privacy laws apply only to doctors, insurers and similar bodies, leaving device makers bound mainly by their own terms of service. Those terms can change, and a company that is sold may take its users' data with it.
Insurers have shown interest in the readings, sometimes offering discounts to customers who share them. Supporters say the arrangement rewards healthy habits. Critics reply that discounts for some soon look like penalties for others, and that people with chronic conditions are the least able to benefit. Comprehensive data protection laws, such as those that treat health information as a special category regardless of who holds it, close part of this gap, but only if they are enforced against companies that do not regard themselves as part of the health sector.
apoio_EN8C: Data protection laws in many countries require organisations to appoint a person responsible for handling questions about personal data. The title varies (data protection officer in Europe, encarregado in Brazil), but the role is broadly similar: to advise the organisation on its obligations, to answer people who want to exercise their rights and to act as a point of contact with the supervisory authority.
The position only works if its holder can speak freely. An officer who reports to the same manager whose projects he or she is supposed to scrutinise may be tempted to stay quiet. For this reason, European rules state that the officer must not be dismissed or penalised for performing the tasks of the role and must report directly to the highest level of management. Conflicts of interest are a related concern: the head of marketing, for instance, is poorly placed to judge whether a new advertising campaign uses customers' data lawfully.
Small organisations often struggle to justify a full-time post. Many therefore share an officer with other bodies or hire an external specialist. This is generally permitted, provided that the person is easily accessible and has enough time and knowledge to do the job properly. What is not permitted is to treat the role as a name on a website with no one behind it.
apoio_EN8D: Should a regulator punish every breach it discovers, or try first to persuade? For a long time, the debate was framed as a choice between these two styles. In an influential book published in 1992, Ian Ayres and John Braithwaite argued that the choice was a false one. Regulators, they suggested, should start with dialogue and escalate only when cooperation fails.
Their proposal is usually drawn as a pyramid. At the broad base sit advice and warnings, which are used most often; higher up come formal notices and fines; at the narrow top lie the harshest sanctions, such as suspending an activity or withdrawing a licence. The top is rarely reached, yet its existence matters: firms are more willing to cooperate at the base when they know that the regulator can, and will, climb further.
Critics point out that the model assumes a regulator with enough resources to monitor firms closely and a credible range of penalties. Where sanctions are weak or slow, a strategy that begins with persuasion may simply invite delay. Even so, the pyramid has shaped enforcement policies in many fields, including data protection, where authorities often combine guidance and warnings with fines reserved for serious or repeated violations.
apoio_EN8E: End-to-end encryption means that only the sender and the recipient of a message can read it; not even the company that runs the service holds the keys. Messaging apps adopted the technology widely in the last decade, and billions of people now use it every day, often without knowing.
Police and intelligence agencies have warned that the trend is making it harder to investigate serious crimes, and some governments have asked companies to build a way for authorities to read messages when a court allows it. Security experts almost unanimously reject the idea. A door built for the police, they argue, can also be found by criminals and hostile states, and there is no way to create a weakness that only the good side can use.
The debate is often presented as privacy against security, but that framing is misleading. Encryption also protects security: it keeps banking details, medical records and trade secrets out of the hands of thieves. Investigators, meanwhile, still have other tools, such as access to the devices themselves, to metadata showing who contacted whom, and to cooperation from people involved in a case. The real question is which risks a society prefers to accept.
