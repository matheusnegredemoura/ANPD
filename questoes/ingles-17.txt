apoio_EN17A: When a company suffers a data breach, the law in many countries requires it to tell the people affected. The rule seems simple, but its effects depend on how the message is written. A notice full of technical terms and legal caveats may satisfy the regulator while leaving customers no wiser about what to do next.
There is also a risk of fatigue. People who receive warnings every few months may start to ignore them, much as drivers stop noticing a road sign they pass every day. Regulators have therefore begun to insist that notices be short, specific and accompanied by practical advice, such as changing passwords or monitoring bank statements.
None of this replaces prevention. A well-written notice limits the damage; it does not undo it.

apoio_EN17B: Governments collect vast amounts of information, from bus timetables to hospital waiting lists. Publishing this material in open formats allows journalists, researchers and entrepreneurs to find uses that officials never imagined. Several successful apps were built entirely on public transport data released by city councils.
Openness, however, must be balanced against privacy. Datasets that look harmless on their own can reveal sensitive details when combined with others. For this reason, agencies are encouraged to assess the risk of re-identification before releasing data about individuals, even when names have been removed.
The aim is not to publish less, but to publish wisely.
