apoio_EN10A: Governments increasingly expect online services to know whether their users are children. The methods vary widely. Self-declaration, in which users simply state their age, is cheap but easily bypassed. Document checks are more reliable, yet they require users to hand over sensitive information to companies they may not trust. Facial age estimation, which guesses age from a selfie, avoids storing documents but raises its own concerns about accuracy across different groups.
Regulators therefore tend to favour a proportionate approach: the greater the risk a service poses to children, the more robust the age check should be. A platform that hosts pornography warrants a different level of assurance than a homework app. Privacy advocates add that age assurance should reveal only what is needed, namely whether a user is above or below a threshold, and not their full identity.
Critics warn that poorly designed checks may push young people towards less regulated corners of the internet. Others reply that doing nothing is also a choice, and one whose costs fall mostly on children.
apoio_EN10B: A regulatory sandbox allows firms to test new products under the supervision of a regulator, often with temporary relief from certain rules. The idea, borrowed from software development, is that problems are easier to spot and fix in a controlled environment than after a product has reached millions of users.
For regulators, sandboxes are also a way of learning. Artificial intelligence moves faster than the rule-making process, and officials rarely have first-hand experience of how models are built and deployed. Working alongside developers gives them evidence on which to base future rules.
Sandboxes are not without risks. Firms that take part may gain a competitive advantage, or an aura of official approval, that rivals lack. And if the relief from rules is too generous, the sandbox may become a loophole rather than a laboratory. Well-run programmes therefore publish clear entry criteria, limit the duration of tests and keep safeguards for the people whose data is used.
apoio_EN10C: When a company discovers that personal data has been exposed, the clock starts ticking. Many laws require organisations to notify the regulator, and sometimes the people affected, within a fixed period. The deadlines differ: the European rule is 72 hours from becoming aware of the breach, while other jurisdictions allow several days or simply require notice "without undue delay".
Short deadlines have a clear purpose. The sooner people know, the sooner they can change passwords, watch their bank accounts or cancel cards. Yet companies complain that, in the first hours after an attack, they rarely know what has been taken. Early notices may therefore be incomplete or even wrong.
Most regimes deal with this by allowing information to be provided in phases. An initial notice sets out what is known; later updates fill in the gaps. What regulators tend to punish is not uncertainty but silence: firms that wait until the full picture is clear, and in the meantime leave customers exposed.
apoio_EN10D: Data flows across borders every second, from cloud backups to customer service centres on other continents. For data protection law, the question is not whether information may leave a country but under what conditions.
The simplest route is an adequacy decision, in which an authority recognises that another country offers a level of protection comparable to its own. Such decisions are convenient but slow to obtain, and they can be withdrawn if the foreign legal framework changes. When no adequacy decision exists, companies usually rely on standard contractual clauses, a set of pre-approved terms that bind the importer of the data to protect it.
Contracts, however, cannot bind a foreign government. Courts in Europe have stressed that firms must assess whether local laws, especially on surveillance, would prevent the importer from honouring its promises. This assessment has turned a routine paperwork exercise into a demanding legal analysis.
