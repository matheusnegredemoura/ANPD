apoio_EN7A: Smart electricity meters were introduced with a simple promise: accurate bills, no more estimated readings and better management of the power grid. Few consumers imagined that the same devices could reveal so much about their private lives. When readings are taken every few minutes, the pattern of consumption shows when a household wakes up, when it is empty and even, in some studies, which television programme is being watched.
Regulators in several countries have responded by limiting how often detailed readings may be collected without the customer's agreement. Monthly totals are usually enough for billing; finer data may be gathered for network planning only after being aggregated or stripped of identifiers. Customers who want personalised advice on saving energy can opt in to share more.
Utilities sometimes object that these rules slow down innovation. Yet surveys suggest that trust is precisely what persuades households to accept new technology. A meter that is perceived as a spy in the kitchen is more likely to be refused than one whose data practices are explained in plain language.
apoio_EN7B: When schools closed during the pandemic, millions of pupils moved their lessons to online platforms almost overnight. Teachers had little time to compare products, and many tools were adopted simply because they were free and easy to use. Only later did researchers begin to ask what happened to the data those platforms collected.
The answers were not always reassuring. Some applications tracked pupils' activity well beyond what was needed for teaching, and a few shared information with advertising companies. Because the tools were chosen by schools, parents were rarely asked for their views, and children themselves had no realistic way to say no.
Education authorities have since tried to restore some balance. Procurement rules increasingly require suppliers to state which data they collect, to use them only for educational purposes and to delete them when the contract ends. Critics welcome these steps but warn that rules on paper mean little unless someone checks whether they are followed in practice.
apoio_EN7C: For decades, a bank customer's transaction history was locked inside the institution that held the account. Open banking changes that arrangement. With the customer's permission, other authorised firms can access the data through secure interfaces and offer services such as budgeting apps, quicker loan decisions or easier switching to a cheaper provider.
Supporters argue that the model gives consumers control over information that describes their own lives and forces established banks to compete harder. The data, after all, was generated by the customer's spending, not by the bank's ingenuity.
The risks are real, however. Each new firm with access is another potential point of failure, and customers may grant permissions without fully understanding how long they last. For this reason, most frameworks require that consent be specific, time-limited and easy to revoke, and they hold the firms that receive the data to the same security standards as banks. Whether consumers will read the permission screens any more carefully than they read cookie banners remains an open question.
apoio_EN7D: Not long ago, faking a convincing video required a film studio. Today, a few minutes of someone's recorded voice and a handful of photographs are enough to produce a clip in which that person appears to say things they never said. The technology has legitimate uses, from dubbing films to restoring the voices of patients who have lost the ability to speak, but its potential for harm is obvious.
Fraudsters have used cloned voices to persuade employees to transfer money, and fabricated intimate images have been used to harass and extort, particularly women and teenagers. During elections, a well-timed fake can spread faster than any correction.
Lawmakers have tried several approaches at once. Some require that synthetic content be clearly labelled; others create specific offences for producing non-consensual intimate images; still others oblige platforms to remove reported material quickly. None of these measures is sufficient on its own. Detection tools improve, but so do the methods for evading them, and a label is of little use if it can be removed with a single click.
apoio_EN7E: Behavioural economists have long observed that people tend to stick with whatever option is presented as the default. Employees enrolled automatically in a pension scheme rarely leave it, while those who must sign up actively often never get round to doing so. The same inertia shapes how we use technology: few users change the settings that come with a new phone or application.
This insight cuts both ways. Governments can use defaults to encourage choices that most people would endorse on reflection, such as saving for retirement or donating organs. Companies, on the other hand, can exploit the same tendency by setting data-sharing options to the most permissive position, knowing that most users will never look.
Data protection law responds by requiring privacy by default: the most protective settings must apply unless the user decides otherwise. Some critics see this as paternalism. Its defenders answer that every product has a default anyway, and the only question is whose interests that default is designed to serve.
