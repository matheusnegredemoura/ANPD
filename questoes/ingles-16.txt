apoio_EN16A: Data protection authorities are expected to supervise not only private companies but also the government itself. This dual role explains why independence is so often described as their most important feature. An authority that depends on the ministry it is supposed to monitor will find it difficult to criticise that ministry, however serious the problem may be.
Independence, however, is not the same as isolation. Authorities must still account for their decisions, publish reports and justify the fines they impose. Courts can review their acts, and parliaments can question their priorities. What independence protects is the freedom to decide each case on its merits, without instructions from those who might be affected by the outcome.
Budget is the less visible side of the issue. An authority that cannot hire experts or afford technical tools may remain formally independent while being, in practice, unable to act.

apoio_EN16B: Lawmakers in several countries now require online platforms to check the age of their users before offering certain services. The goal is easy to defend: children should not be exposed to content and features designed for adults. The method is harder to get right.
The most reliable checks often rely on official documents or facial analysis, which means collecting more personal data, sometimes from everyone, adults included. A measure intended to protect privacy can thus end up eroding it. For this reason, many experts favour solutions in which a trusted service confirms only that a user is above or below a certain age, without revealing who that person is.
No method is perfect. The challenge for regulators is to require checks proportionate to the risk: a video game rated for all ages hardly needs the same scrutiny as a gambling website.
