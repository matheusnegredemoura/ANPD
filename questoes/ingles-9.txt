apoio_EN9A: Around the world, governments are building digital identity systems that allow citizens to prove who they are online, open bank accounts and claim benefits without queuing at an office. Supporters point to India, where a single biometric identifier now covers most of the population, and to Estonia, where almost every public service can be completed online.
The benefits are real, but so are the dangers. A single identifier makes it easy to link records that used to be kept apart, from health files to tax returns. If that identifier leaks, the damage is hard to undo: a password can be changed, but a fingerprint cannot. Critics also warn that people without documents, stable internet access or legible fingerprints, such as manual workers and the elderly, may eventually be shut out of services they are entitled to.
Well-designed systems try to address these risks. They collect only the attributes needed for each transaction, allow citizens to see who has consulted their data and keep offline alternatives for those who cannot use digital channels. Whether a digital ID empowers or excludes depends less on the technology than on these choices.
apoio_EN9B: Online shops have always adjusted prices to demand, raising them before holidays and cutting them when stock piles up. What is new is the ability to set a different price for each customer. By analysing browsing history, location and even the type of device used, an algorithm can estimate how much a particular shopper is willing to pay and charge accordingly.
Economists call this first-degree price discrimination, and its effects are ambiguous. In some cases it allows firms to serve customers who would otherwise be priced out, since those with less money may be offered discounts. In others, it simply transfers value from consumers to sellers, who capture almost all of the gain from each sale.
Consumers tend to find the practice unfair once they discover it, especially when the factors used seem unrelated to cost. For this reason, several authorities have argued that personalised prices should at least be disclosed. Data protection law adds another constraint: if the price depends on a profile built from personal data, the customer may have the right to know the criteria used and to challenge automated decisions that significantly affect them.
apoio_EN9C: Large platforms remove millions of posts every month, most of them flagged by automated systems before any user has seen them. For years, the public knew little about how these decisions were made. Transparency reports, first published voluntarily by a few companies, were meant to change that by revealing how many items were removed, for what reasons and at whose request.
The early reports were easy to praise and hard to use. Each company chose its own categories and methods, so figures could not be compared, and numbers alone said nothing about whether the right content had been taken down. A platform could report that it had removed more hate speech than ever without anyone knowing whether that reflected better detection or simply more hate speech.
New laws in several jurisdictions now require standardised reports, independent audits and access to data for vetted researchers. The goal is not to dictate what may be said but to make the systems that shape public debate open to scrutiny. Platforms warn that sharing data with researchers may itself create privacy risks, a concern that regulators acknowledge but do not consider a reason for secrecy.
apoio_EN9D: Attackers have learned that the easiest way into a well-defended organisation is often through someone it trusts. Instead of breaking into a bank or a ministry directly, they compromise a software supplier and hide malicious code in a routine update. When thousands of customers install the update, the attackers gain access to all of them at once.
Such supply chain attacks are hard to detect precisely because they exploit legitimate channels. The update carries a valid signature, comes from the expected server and behaves normally for weeks. By the time anyone notices, the intruders may have moved deep into the networks they reached.
Defenders have responded by demanding more information about the components inside the software they buy, often in the form of a "software bill of materials", a list of every library and dependency used. Others limit what each application is allowed to do, so that a compromised program cannot reach everything. None of these measures removes the need for trust, but they reduce how much damage a single broken link can cause.
apoio_EN9E: Regulators need people who understand the industries they oversee, and the best place to find such expertise is often the industry itself. The same logic works in reverse: companies value former officials who know how the agency thinks. The resulting traffic between public and private posts, known as the revolving door, is not in itself corrupt, but it raises an obvious question about whose interests an official is serving.
Researchers have found mixed evidence. Some studies suggest that officials who hope to join a firm later may treat it more gently while still in office. Others find the opposite effect: officials trying to impress future employers may act tougher, to demonstrate competence. What seems clearer is that regulators who come from and return to the same small group of companies are likely to share its assumptions, even without any improper intent.
Most countries therefore rely on cooling-off periods, during which former officials may not work for, or lobby, the bodies they used to regulate. Such rules are a reasonable compromise, but they work only if they are enforced and if the periods are long enough to matter.
