apoio_EN11A: Most people have never heard of the companies that know the most about them. Data brokers buy information from apps, loyalty programmes and public records, combine it into detailed profiles and sell those profiles to advertisers, insurers and, increasingly, to anyone willing to pay. Because they rarely deal with consumers directly, they face little of the pressure that keeps better-known firms in check.
Regulators have struggled to respond. Each piece of data may have been collected lawfully, with some form of consent buried in a privacy notice, yet the combined profile can reveal far more than any individual source: a person's health condition, religion or financial distress. The harm, in other words, often lies not in the collection but in the aggregation.
Some lawmakers now propose registries that would require brokers to disclose who they are and allow people to opt out of all of them at once. Critics warn that such registries could themselves become a target for attackers. Supporters reply that the alternative, a market no one can see, is riskier still.
apoio_EN11B: For years, online safety for children meant warning parents and asking teenagers to be careful. The new approach shifts the burden to the companies that design the services. Instead of expecting a twelve-year-old to navigate dozens of privacy settings, it requires that the most protective options be switched on by default.
The idea draws on a simple observation: few users ever change default settings, and children are even less likely to do so. A feature that is off unless someone turns it on will reach only a small minority; a feature that is on unless someone turns it off will reach almost everyone. Design, in short, is policy by other means.
Not everyone is convinced. Some developers argue that strict defaults make products less engaging and push young users towards services that ignore the rules. Regulators counter that engagement has often been the problem rather than the goal, and that a service unable to attract children without exploiting them should not be targeting them at all.
apoio_EN11C: An independent regulator is only as credible as its decisions are understandable. When an authority publishes the reasoning behind a fine, explains which factors weighed most and shows how the amount was calculated, companies can predict how rules will be applied and adjust before problems arise. When it does not, even a correct decision may look arbitrary.
Transparency also disciplines the regulator itself. Officials who know that their reasoning will be read by courts, journalists and the regulated industry tend to write more carefully and to treat similar cases alike. Secrecy, by contrast, makes inconsistency easier to hide.
There is a cost, of course. Detailed explanations take time, and some information, such as trade secrets or details of ongoing investigations, cannot be disclosed. The challenge is not to choose between openness and confidentiality but to draw the line between them in a way that can itself be explained.
