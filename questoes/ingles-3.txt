// Textos originais escritos para o banco, em inglês, sobre temas da ANPD (decisões automatizadas, sandbox
// regulatório e notificação de incidentes). Itens em português, texto em inglês, como na prova Cebraspe.

apoio_EN3A: Automated decision-making is no longer confined to laboratories. Banks use it to approve loans, employers to screen applicants, and insurers to set premiums. Its defenders argue that algorithms are more consistent than human beings, who are prone to fatigue and prejudice. Critics reply that consistency is not the same as fairness: a model trained on biased historical data will reproduce that bias at scale, and with an appearance of neutrality that makes it harder to contest.
For this reason, most data protection laws do not ban automated decisions outright. Instead, they grant individuals the right to obtain an explanation and to request a review. The difficulty lies in what counts as an explanation. Companies are reluctant to disclose their models, which they regard as trade secrets, while regulators insist that people affected by a decision deserve to understand, at least, the main criteria behind it.
Some authorities have found a middle ground: rather than forcing firms to publish their code, they reserve the power to audit systems themselves whenever a firm refuses to explain its choices. Whether this arrangement will prove effective remains to be seen.
apoio_EN3B: Regulators are often accused of being too slow for the technologies they oversee. By the time a rule is drafted, debated and approved, the market it was meant to govern may have moved on. Regulatory sandboxes were conceived as a response to this mismatch. In a sandbox, a firm is allowed to test an innovative product with real customers for a limited period, under close supervision and with some requirements temporarily relaxed.
The benefits are said to run both ways. Firms gain legal certainty while they experiment, and regulators learn how new technologies work before deciding how to treat them. Nevertheless, sandboxes are not free of risks. If the selection of participants is not transparent, they may favour incumbents over newcomers; if exit rules are vague, a temporary exemption may quietly become permanent.
Data protection authorities have recently joined this trend, particularly in the field of artificial intelligence, where the gap between innovation and regulation seems widest.
apoio_EN3C: When a company discovers that personal data in its custody have been exposed, the clock starts ticking. Many jurisdictions now require organisations to notify both the supervisory authority and the affected individuals within a short period after becoming aware of the incident. The rationale is straightforward: the sooner people know, the sooner they can change passwords, monitor their bank accounts or take other protective steps.
Yet speed has a cost. In the first hours after an attack, companies seldom know how many people were affected or which data were taken. Notifications sent too early may be incomplete; those sent too late may be useless. Several laws address this dilemma by allowing an initial notice to be supplemented later, as long as the delay is justified.
Notification, however, is not an end in itself. Regulators increasingly treat the way a company responds to an incident, rather than the incident alone, as the real test of its accountability.
